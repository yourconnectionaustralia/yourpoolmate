Pool water, explained

Straight answers to the questions every owner asks.

Why does my pool keep going green?

Green water is almost always algae. It takes hold when free chlorine drops below 1.0 ppm — usually because strong UV, heavy use, warm water or low stabiliser (cyanuric acid) burns the chlorine off faster than it's replaced.

The fix is to shock with a high dose of chlorine, brush the walls and floor, and run the filter until it clears, which can take several days. Testing weekly and keeping stabiliser at 30–50 ppm is what stops it coming back.

Is my pool safe for children and grandchildren to swim in?

Safe means free chlorine between 1.0 and 3.0 ppm, pH between 7.2 and 7.6, and clear water. Below 1.0 ppm chlorine the water isn't properly sanitised; above 5.0 ppm it can irritate eyes and skin.

Clear water can still be short of chlorine, so the only way to know is to test. Your Pool Mate gives a plain safe-to-swim or hold-off call from your latest test.

What should my pool's chlorine level be?

Free chlorine should sit between 1.0 and 3.0 ppm. In an uncovered outdoor pool with stabiliser (30–50 ppm), aim for about 3.0 ppm — stabiliser holds some chlorine back, and NSW public-pool rules use 3.0 ppm for stabilised outdoor pools.

Keep your pool under a cover? Many covered pools run without stabiliser, and 1.5–2.0 ppm is plenty there and kinder to the cover. Combined chlorine (total minus free) should stay under 0.2 ppm; at 0.5 ppm or more the pool needs shocking.

What does pool pH mean and why does it matter?

pH measures how acidic or alkaline the water is, on a 0–14 scale. The right range is 7.2–7.6. Low pH stings eyes, corrodes equipment and burns through chlorine. High pH makes chlorine far less effective and causes cloudiness and scale.

What is total alkalinity and why does it need to be correct?

Total alkalinity (80–120 ppm) is the water's ability to resist pH swings. Too low and pH jumps around with rain, chemicals or heavy use. Too high and pH is hard to bring down, with cloudy water as a side effect. Correct alkalinity first, then fine-tune pH.

Why is my pool cloudy even though I've added chlorine?

Usually one of three things: high pH making the chlorine less effective, not enough filtration, or high combined chlorine (chloramines) that means the pool needs shocking. Test every reading, not just chlorine — more chlorine in water at pH 8.0 won't do much.

How often should I test my pool water?

At least weekly in spring and summer, twice a week in heavy use, very hot weather or after rain. Fortnightly is usually enough in winter. Regular testing is the simplest way to avoid big corrective doses — and it builds your record.

Can I use my pool the same day I add chemicals?

After chlorine or pH adjusters, wait at least four hours with the pump running, then re-test. After shocking, wait until free chlorine is back under 5.0 ppm. Always follow the product label.

Why do I need a record of my pool water tests?

Pool builder and equipment warranties can ask for evidence the water was looked after, and a dated record helps when you sell the house. Pool shops rarely give you a copy you can keep.
