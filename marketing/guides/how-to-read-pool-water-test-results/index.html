How to read your pool shop water test

Free chlorine, pH, alkalinity, stabiliser, calcium, salt: what each reading on the printout means, what it should be, and the order to fix them in.

By the Your Pool Mate teamUpdated 23 November 2026

The short answer

Read a pool water test in this order: free chlorine (1.0–3.0 ppm) tells you if the water is sanitised, pH (7.2–7.6) tells you if that chlorine is working, and total alkalinity (80–120 ppm) tells you if pH will hold. Stabiliser, calcium hardness and salt change slowly and come next.

  • Free chlorine 1.0–3.0 ppm
  • pH 7.2–7.6
  • Total alkalinity 80–120 ppm
  • Stabiliser (cyanuric acid) 30–50 ppm
  • Calcium hardness 200–400 ppm (vinyl: up to 400)
  • Salt: whatever your chlorinator's manual says, usually 3,000–5,000 ppm

The ideal range for every reading

These are the ranges most Australian pool shops and equipment makers work to, and the ones Your Pool Mate scores against. Anything inside the range is fine. You don't need to chase the middle.

Low-salt and mineral chlorinators run far lower salt than a standard cell. Check the model plate or manual before you add salt.

Which reading to fix first

Pool shops often hand over a list of five products at once. You'll usually spend less, and get better results, fixing things in this order and re-testing between steps:

  1. Total alkalinity. If it's out, pH won't stay put, so sort it first.
  2. pH. Bring it into 7.2–7.6 once alkalinity is right.
  3. Free chlorine. Now the chlorine you add will actually work.
  4. Stabiliser, calcium and salt. These move slowly. Adjust them on a calm day, not in a crisis.

The exception is a green or unsafe pool. If free chlorine is near zero, chlorinate straight away and balance afterwards. See how to fix a green pool.

Free chlorine vs total chlorine

Free chlorine is the chlorine still on duty. Total chlorine adds the chlorine that has already reacted with sweat, sunscreen and leaves (combined chlorine, or chloramines). Subtract free from total to get combined chlorine.

  • Combined under 0.2 ppm: normal.
  • Combined 0.5 ppm or more: the pool needs a shock dose to burn it off. This is the "chlorine smell" people blame on too much chlorine. It's actually too little.

Test strips, drop kits and pool shop tests

Test strips are quick and fine for weekly checks of chlorine and pH. Read them in shade, at the time on the bottle, against the bottle's own chart. Drop (reagent) kits are more precise for chlorine and alkalinity. A pool shop test uses a photometer and is the most complete, including calcium, salt and phosphates.

A good routine for most home pools: strips or drops every week in summer, plus a full shop test every month or two. Keep every result. Patterns over weeks tell you far more than a single reading.

Keep the printout (or a photo of it)

Most shops keep your results on their system, not yours. If you ever need to show how the water was looked after (for an equipment warranty, a builder, or a buyer when you sell), a dated history of your own is what counts. See pool warranty water records.

Common questions

What does ppm mean on a pool test?

Parts per million. 1 ppm of chlorine is 1 gram of chlorine in 1,000 litres (1 kL) of water. Phosphates are often shown in ppb, parts per billion.

Why does my pool shop test differ from my test strips?

Strips are less precise, colours are hard to judge in bright sun, and strips past their use-by date drift. Small differences (half a ppm of chlorine, 0.2 of pH) are normal. Big differences mean an old strip or a sample that sat too long before testing.

How often should I get a pool shop water test?

Every four to eight weeks in the swimming season is plenty for most home pools, with your own strip or drop test every week in between. Test more often after heavy rain, a party or a heatwave.
