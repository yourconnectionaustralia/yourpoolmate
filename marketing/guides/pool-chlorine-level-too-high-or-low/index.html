Pool chlorine too high or too low

Free chlorine should sit between 1.0 and 3.0 ppm. Here's how to bring it back when it isn't, and why sun and stabiliser change the answer.

The short answer

Keep free chlorine between 1.0 and 3.0 ppm. Aim for about 3 ppm in an uncovered outdoor pool with stabiliser, and 1.5–2.0 ppm in a covered pool. Too low: add liquid chlorine (about 8 mL per 1,000 L per ppm) or turn your chlorinator up. Too high: stop adding chlorine and let sun and time bring it down. Don't swim above 5 ppm.

  • Range: 1.0–3.0 ppm free chlorine
  • Uncovered + stabiliser: aim about 3 ppm
  • Covered pool: 1.5–2.0 ppm
  • Low: add chlorine or raise chlorinator output
  • High: stop adding, leave the cover off, wait

What's the right chlorine level?

The acceptable range is 1.0–3.0 ppm of free chlorine. Where to aim inside it depends on your pool:

  • Uncovered outdoor pool with stabiliser (30–50 ppm): aim for about 3 ppm. Stabiliser protects chlorine from the sun but also holds some back, so you need a bit more. NSW public-pool guidelines use 3 ppm for stabilised outdoor pools.
  • Pool under a cover, little or no stabiliser: 1.5–2.0 ppm is plenty, and kinder to the cover.

Australian summer sun burns chlorine fast. Queensland and the NT in summer need more chlorine per day than Hobart in spring.

Chlorine too low

Under 1 ppm, the water isn't reliably sanitised and algae can start within days.

  1. Check pH first. If it's above 7.8, lower it, or the chlorine you add will be weak.
  2. Add chlorine. Liquid chlorine (about 12.5%) lifts free chlorine by roughly 1 ppm per 8 mL per 1,000 L. A 50,000 L pool from 1 to 3 ppm needs about 800 mL. Our chlorine dose calculator works it out for you.
  3. Salt pool? Turn the chlorinator output up or run the pump longer, and check the salt level and the cell for scale.
  4. Keeps dropping overnight? Something is using it up, usually algae starting, debris, or a lot of swimmers. Shock and brush.

Chlorine too high

Above 3 ppm isn't dangerous at first, but above about 5 ppm it irritates eyes and skin and fades swimwear and liners.

  • Stop adding chlorine and turn the chlorinator down or off.
  • Take the cover off and let the sun work. A sunny day can burn off several ppm.
  • Run the pump as normal and re-test the next day.
  • For a big overdose, a partial drain and refill is the fastest fix. Chlorine neutralisers exist, but they're easy to overdo.

"It smells of chlorine, so there must be too much"

Usually the opposite. That sharp pool smell is combined chlorine (chloramines), chlorine that has been used up. If total chlorine is 0.5 ppm or more above free chlorine, the pool needs a shock dose. See how to read your test.

When can I swim after adding chlorine?

After a normal dose of liquid chlorine, wait at least four hours with the pump running, then re-test. After a shock, wait until free chlorine is back under 5 ppm. Always follow the product label.

Common questions

Is 5 ppm chlorine safe to swim in?

Around 5 ppm is the upper limit many guidelines use. Most people find it uncomfortable on eyes and skin. Wait until it drops into the 1–3 ppm range.

Why won't my salt pool hold chlorine?

Common reasons: low salt, a scaled or worn cell, the output set too low, too few pump hours, low stabiliser in summer sun, or algae starting. Check salt and the cell first.

Liquid chlorine or granular?

Both work. Liquid chlorine adds nothing but chlorine and a little salt. Granular calcium hypochlorite also adds calcium, which suits pools with low calcium but can push already-hard water into scale.
